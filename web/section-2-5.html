    Section Section 2.5 Applications of Linear Equations

    Subsection Linear Equations Review

    Definition 83. Linear Equation.

    An equation that can be written as \(Ax + By = C\text{,}\) where \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) \(C\) are real numbers and \(A\) and \(B\) are not both \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Point-Slope Form

    Recall the slope formula:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{y - y_1}{x - x_1} = m \end{equation*}
    Multiplying both sides by \(x - x_1\) gives the point-slope form of a line:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y - y_1 = m(x - x_1) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This form is useful when you know the slope of a line and one point on the line.
    🔗
    While the slope-intercept form \(y = mx + b\) will satisfy most linear needs, there are times where the point-slope form makes things easier.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

    Parallel and Perpendicular Lines Video
    🔗

    Definition 84. Parallel Lines.

    Parallel lines have matching slopes.
    🔗
    If \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) represent the slopes of two nonvertical parallel lines, then \(m_1 = m_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 85. Perpendicular Lines.

    Perpendicular lines have slopes that are negative reciprocals.
    🔗
    If \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) represent the slopes of two nonvertical perpendicular lines, then:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} m_1 = -\frac{1}{m_2} \end{equation*}
    In other words, \(m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1\text{,}\) or they are negative reciprocals of each other.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 99.

    The slope of a line is given. Determine the slope of a line parallel and perpendicular to the given line, if possible.
    🔗
    1. \(m = \dfrac{1}{2}\)
    🔗
    2. \(m = -4\)
    🔗
    3. \(m = 0\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(m = \dfrac{1}{2}\)
    🔗
    Parallel slope: \(m = \dfrac{1}{2}\)
    🔗
    Perpendicular slope: \(m = -2\) (negative reciprocal of \(\frac{1}{2}\) is \(-2\))
    🔗
    2. \(m = -4\)
    🔗
    Parallel slope: \(m = -4\)
    🔗
    Perpendicular slope: \(m = \dfrac{1}{4}\) (negative reciprocal of \(-4\) is \(\frac{1}{4}\))
    🔗
    3. \(m = 0\)
    🔗
    Parallel slope: \(m = 0\) (horizontal lines are parallel)
    🔗
    Perpendicular slope: undefined (vertical line). A horizontal line is perpendicular to a vertical line.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 100.

    Example. Give the equations of two lines that pass through \((-1, 1)\text{.}\) The first is parallel and the second perpendicular to the line defined by \(y = -2x + 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint: What do we need from the equation to the left?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Step 1: Identify the slope of the given line.
    🔗
    The line \(y = -2x + 5\) has slope \(m = -2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: Find the slope of the parallel line.
    🔗
    Parallel lines have the same slope: \(m = -2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using point-slope form with \((-1, 1)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y - 1 = -2(x - (-1)) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y - 1 = -2(x + 1) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y - 1 = -2x - 2 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y = -2x - 1 \end{equation*}
    The parallel line is \(y = -2x - 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3: Find the slope of the perpendicular line.
    🔗
    Perpendicular slopes are negative reciprocals. Since \(m = -2\text{,}\) the perpendicular slope is \(m = \frac{1}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using point-slope form with \((-1, 1)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y - 1 = \frac{1}{2}(x - (-1)) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y - 1 = \frac{1}{2}(x + 1) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y - 1 = \frac{1}{2}x + \frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y = \frac{1}{2}x + \frac{3}{2} \end{equation*}
    The perpendicular line is \(y = \frac{1}{2}x + \frac{3}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Linear Cost, Revenue, and Profit Functions

    What does it cost to produce something for sale? What is the difference between the costs of the building and equipment, and the costs of material, energy, and labor?
    🔗
    A linear cost function models the cost \(C(x)\) to produce \(x\) items:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} C(x) = mx + b \end{equation*}
    where:
    🔗
    • \(x\) is the number of items produced.
      🔗
    • \(b\) is the fixed cost – the cost that does not change in relation to the number of items produced. It includes expenses like office space, equipment, and insurance.
      🔗
    • \(m\) is the variable cost – the direct cost to produce each item. It includes materials, energy, and labor.
      🔗
    A linear revenue function models revenue \(R(x)\) for selling \(x\) items:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R(x) = px \end{equation*}
    where:
    🔗
    • \(x\) is the number of items sold.
      🔗
    • \(p\) is the selling price per item.
      🔗
    A linear profit function models the profit for producing and selling \(x\) items:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(x) = R(x) - C(x) \end{equation*}
    Profit is Revenue minus Cost.
    🔗

    Definition 87. Break-Even Point.

    The break-even point occurs when profit is zero, i.e., \(P(x) = 0\text{.}\) At this point, \(R(x) = C(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 101.

    Example. A company invests \(\$18{,}000\) to produce snow boots for dogs. Each set of boots costs \(\$5\) to manufacture, package, and ship, and will sell for \(\$20\text{.}\)
    🔗
    a. What is the Cost function?
    🔗
    b. What is the Revenue function?
    🔗
    c. What is the Profit function?
    🔗
    d. What is the profit if they produce and sell 1,000 sets of boots?
    🔗
    e. What is the profit if they produce and sell 2,000 sets of boots?
    🔗
    f. How many sets do they need to produce and sell to make \(\$0\) profit? What is this number called?
    🔗
    Solution.
    a. The fixed cost is \(b = 18000\) (the initial investment). The variable cost is \(m = 5\) (cost per set).
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} C(x) = 5x + 18000 \end{equation*}
    b. The selling price is \(p = 20\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R(x) = 20x \end{equation*}
    c. Profit is Revenue minus Cost.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(x) = R(x) - C(x) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} P(x) = 20x - (5x + 18000) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} P(x) = 15x - 18000 \end{equation*}
    d. For \(x = 1000\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(1000) = 15(1000) - 18000 = 15000 - 18000 = -3000 \end{equation*}
    The company loses \(\$3{,}000\) (negative profit).
    🔗
    e. For \(x = 2000\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(2000) = 15(2000) - 18000 = 30000 - 18000 = 12000 \end{equation*}
    The company makes \(\$12{,}000\) profit.
    🔗
    f. Set \(P(x) = 0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 15x - 18000 = 0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 15x = 18000 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x = 1200 \end{equation*}
    The company needs to produce and sell \(1200\) sets of boots to break even. This is called the break-even point.
    🔗
