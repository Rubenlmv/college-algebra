    Section Section 3.3 Polynomial Division

    Subsection Polynomial Division

    In this section, we will study two procedures for dividing polynomials.
    🔗
    Do you remember long division?
    🔗
    Example: \(6170 \div 5\)
    🔗

    Problem-Solving Strategy 196. Long Division Steps.

    • Divide the first digit by the divisor (5). Write the result on top (1).
      🔗
    • Multiply the result from step 1 by 5 and write below the dividend.
      🔗
    • Subtract (6 − 5) and "bring down" the next digit.
      🔗
    • Divide the result from step 3 by the divisor and back to step 3.
      🔗
    • Repeat until no digits remain.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Because \(6170 \div 5 = 1234\) (with remainder 0), we say that 5 is a factor of 6170.
    🔗
    That is: \(5 \cdot 1234 = 6170\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Division Algorithm

    Definition 198. Division Algorithm.

    Suppose that \(f(x)\) and \(d(x)\) are polynomials where \(d(x) \neq 0\) and the degree of \(d(x)\) is less than or equal to the degree of \(f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then there exists unique polynomials \(q(x)\) and \(r(x)\) such that:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = d(x) \cdot q(x) + r(x) \end{equation*}
    or
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{f(x)}{d(x)} = q(x) + \frac{r(x)}{d(x)} \end{equation*}
    where the degree of \(r(x)\) is zero or of lesser degree than \(d(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Note: The polynomial \(f(x)\) is the dividend, \(d(x)\) is the divisor, \(q(x)\) is the quotient, and \(r(x)\) is the remainder.
    🔗
    This means that we can divide one polynomial by another of lesser degree. BUT HOW?
    🔗
    Dividing Polynomials Using Long Division Video
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Dividing Polynomials Using Long Division

    Problem-Solving Strategy 199. Long Division Strategy.

    • To begin the division: Always write the terms of the dividend and divisor in descending order with place holders for missing powers of \(x\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Divide the FIRST TERM of the dividend by the FIRST TERM of the divisor. Place the result on the top of the division symbol.
      🔗
    • Multiply the entire divisor by the new term in the quotient and write the result under the dividend.
      🔗
    • Subtract and bring down the next term.
      🔗
    • Repeat until the difference is of lower degree than the divisor (or zero).
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 132.

    Use Polynomial Long Division to divide \(\left(2x^3 - 11x^2 + 13x + 15\right) \div \left(2x - 5\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Thus, we can write the result in two equivalent forms:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2x^3 - 11x^2 + 13x + 15 = (2x - 5)(x^2 - 3x - 1) + 10 \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2x^3 - 11x^2 + 13x + 15}{2x - 5} = x^2 - 3x - 1 + \frac{10}{2x - 5}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 133.

    Use Polynomial Division to divide \(\left(x^5 - 3x^4 + 12x - 33\right) \div \left(x - 3\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^5 - 3x^4 + 12x - 33 = (x - 3)(x^4 + 12) + 3 \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^5 - 3x^4 + 12x - 33}{x - 3} = x^4 + 12 + \frac{3}{x - 3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 134.

    Divide: \(\left(2x^3 + 2x^2 - 16x + 32\right) \div \left(x^2 - 3x + 4\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the remainder is 0, we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2x^3 + 2x^2 - 16x + 32 = (x^2 - 3x + 4)(2x + 8) \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2x^3 + 2x^2 - 16x + 32}{x^2 - 3x + 4} = 2x + 8. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Dividing Polynomials Using Synthetic Division

    Note: I usually skip this topic. Long division always works. But you can review this video if you like using Synthetic Division. Careful, synthetic division only works when you divide by \((x - c)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Dividing Polynomials Using Synthetic Division Video
    🔗
    When dividing polynomials where the divisor is a binomial of the form \((x - c)\) and \(c\) is a constant, we can use synthetic division.
    🔗

    Problem-Solving Strategy 203. Synthetic Division Strategy.

    • Write the value of \(c\) in a box.
      🔗
    • Write the coefficients of the dividend to the right of the box.
      🔗
    • Skip a line and draw a horizontal line below the list of coefficients.
      🔗
    • Bring down the leading coefficient from the dividend and write it below the line.
      🔗
    • Multiply the value of \(c\) by the number below the line. Write the result in the next column above the line.
      🔗
    • Add the numbers in the column above the line, and write the result below the line.
      🔗
    🔗
    Repeat steps 5 and 6 until all columns have been completed. The right-most number below the line is the remainder. The other numbers below the line are the coefficients of the quotient in order by the degree of the term. Since the divisor is linear (1st-degree), the degree of the quotient is 1 less than the degree of the dividend.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 135.

    Divide using synthetic division: \(\left(x^3 - 13x^2 + 46x - 49\right) \div \left(x - 8\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{c|rrrr} 8 & 1 & -13 & 46 & -49 \\ & & 8 & -40 & 48 \\ \hline & 1 & -5 & 6 & \boxed{-1} \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The remainder is \(-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^3 - 13x^2 + 46x - 49 = (x - 8)(x^2 - 5x + 6) - 1 \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^3 - 13x^2 + 46x - 49}{x - 8} = x^2 - 5x + 6 - \frac{1}{x - 8}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 136.

    Divide using synthetic division: \(\left(3x^3 + 25x^2 - 48x + 20\right) \div \left(x + 10\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{c|rrrr} -10 & 3 & 25 & -48 & 20 \\ & & -30 & 50 & -20 \\ \hline & 3 & -5 & 2 & \boxed{0} \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The remainder is 0, so \((x + 10)\) is a factor.
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x^3 + 25x^2 - 48x + 20 = (x + 10)(3x^2 - 5x + 2) \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3x^3 + 25x^2 - 48x + 20}{x + 10} = 3x^2 - 5x + 2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Factor Theorem

    Factor Theorem Video
    🔗
    \(x - c\) is a factor of \(f(x)\) if and only if \(x = c\) is a root of the equation \(f(x) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    What does this mean?
    🔗
    • If you know a factor, you know a zero (root, x-intercept).
      🔗
    • If you know a zero, you know a factor.
      🔗
    Example Video
    🔗

    Example 137.

    Given that 3 and \(-2\) are roots of \(f(x) = 0\text{,}\) construct a polynomial \(f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    If 3 and \(-2\) are roots, then \((x - 3)\) and \((x + 2)\) are factors.
    🔗
    So \(f(x) = (x - 3)(x + 2) = x^2 - x - 6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (Any nonzero constant multiple of this polynomial would also work.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 138.

    Use the factor theorem to determine if \((x - 1)\) is a factor of \(f(x) = 2x^3 + 4x^2 - 2x - 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Evaluate \(f(1)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(1) = 2(1)^3 + 4(1)^2 - 2(1) - 4 = 2 + 4 - 2 - 4 = 0 \end{equation*}
    Since \(f(1) = 0\text{,}\) \((x - 1)\) is a factor of \(f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Remember: If \(f(c) = 0\text{,}\) then:
    🔗
    • \(c\) is a zero of the function
      🔗
    • \(c\) is a solution (or root) of the equation
      🔗
    • \(c\) is an \(x\)-intercept of the graph of \(f(x)\)
      🔗
    • \((x - c)\) is a factor of \(f(x)\) (divides with no remainder)
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Putting It All Together

    Example 139.

    Use the factor theorem to show \((x + 4)\) is a factor of \(f(x) = 6x^3 + 11x^2 - 60x - 32\text{.}\) Then, factor completely, find ALL the zeros and sketch a graph.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Step 1: Verify \((x + 4)\) is a factor.
    🔗
    Evaluate \(f(-4)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(-4) = 6(-4)^3 + 11(-4)^2 - 60(-4) - 32 = -384 + 176 + 240 - 32 = 0 \end{equation*}
    Since \(f(-4) = 0\text{,}\) \((x + 4)\) is a factor.
    🔗
    Step 2: Divide.
    🔗
    Using synthetic division:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{c|rrrr} -4 & 6 & 11 & -60 & -32 \\ & & -24 & 52 & 32 \\ \hline & 6 & -13 & -8 & 0 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    So \(f(x) = (x + 4)(6x^2 - 13x - 8)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3: Factor completely.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 6x^2 - 13x - 8 = (2x + 1)(3x - 8) \end{equation*}
    Therefore:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = (x + 4)(2x + 1)(3x - 8) \end{equation*}
    Step 4: End Behavior.
    🔗
    Degree 3 (odd), leading coefficient 6 (positive) → falls to the left, rises to the right.
    🔗
    Step 5: Zeros.
    🔗
    • \(x = -4\text{,}\) multiplicity 1 (crosses)
      🔗
    • \(x = -\frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) multiplicity 1 (crosses)
      🔗
    • \(x = \frac{8}{3}\text{,}\) multiplicity 1 (crosses)
      🔗
    Step 6: y-intercept.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(0) = -32 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
